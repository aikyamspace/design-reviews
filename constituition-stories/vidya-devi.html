The State must not take your land without authority of law and compensation, and it cannot keep it by claiming adverse possession or delay.

Vidya Devi, an elderly widow, won against the State of Himachal Pradesh. The Supreme Court held that the State took her land for a road without any legal sanction, and that it could not rely on adverse possession or delay to keep it. It ordered compensation under Article 300A.

What happened

Vidya Devi was an almost 80-year-old illiterate widow from a rural background. She owned about 3.34 hectares of land in Mauja Jalari, in Tehsil Nadaun, Himachal Pradesh.

In 1967–68 the State took over her land to build the Nadaun–Sujanpur road. It did not start acquisition proceedings or follow due process. She lost her land and received no compensation. The road was completed by 1975.

Vidya Devi did not claim compensation. She was an illiterate rural widow and was unaware of her legal rights.

In 2004 other landowners whose land had been taken for the same road, including Anakh Singh, filed a writ petition in the High Court claiming compensation. On 23 April 2007 the High Court allowed their petition. It directed the State to acquire their lands under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. In 2008 the State began acquisition, but only for those petitioners. The other landowners, including Vidya Devi, were left out.

She learnt of these proceedings in 2010. With her two daughters she filed a writ petition seeking compensation or the start of acquisition proceedings. The State admitted that it had used her land. It argued that 42 years' possession amounted to adverse possession, that the remedy was a civil suit, that the petition was barred by laches (unreasonable delay), and that she had orally consented.

On 11 September 2013 the High Court held that disputed questions of law and fact on limitation could not be decided in writ proceedings. It gave her liberty to file a civil suit, so she got no relief. Her review petition was dismissed on 13 May 2014.

She then appealed to the Supreme Court. On 8 January 2020 the Court allowed her appeals.

The Court said her property right was a fundamental right in 1967 and is now a human right and a constitutional right under Article 300A. She could not be dispossessed without legal sanction, due process and just compensation (paras 10.1–10.3). The State's claim of oral consent was baseless, and there was no authority or legal sanction for taking her land (para 10.4). In a democracy under the rule of law, a welfare State must follow the lawful procedure for acquisition and cannot place itself beyond the Constitution (para 10.5). A welfare State cannot plead adverse possession, which suits a trespasser, to perfect its title and grab its own citizens' property (para 10.6). Delay and laches cannot be raised where the cause of action is continuing or the circumstances shock the judicial conscience, and a constitutional court acts to promote justice (para 10.7).

The Court noted that the cause of action was continuing. She had received no compensation for over half a century, and the State admitted taking the land without any procedure known to law, so the demand for justice was compelling (para 11). The State had acted only for those landowners who had obtained High Court directions, forcing the others to go to court individually (para 12).

The Court set aside the High Court orders. It directed the State to pay her compensation on the same terms as in Anakh Singh's case, with solatium, interest and other statutory benefits, within 8 weeks, treating the land as deemed acquired. The State must file an affidavit of compliance within 10 weeks and pay her costs. If she appeals within 8 weeks of receiving the compensation, the appeal will be treated as within limitation (paras 13–14).

The law in plain words

Article 300A says no person can be deprived of property except by authority of law. In everyday words, the government cannot simply take your land. It must use a law, such as the acquisition procedure, and pay compensation. The Court said a State cannot claim ownership just by holding land for many years, because adverse possession suits a trespasser and not a welfare State (para 10.6). It also cannot hide behind delay when the wrong continues (para 10.7). The protection applies only to deprivation of property. A taking done under a valid law, following its procedure, is not barred by this Article.

What you can do

If the government has taken your land without acquisition proceedings or compensation, get legal advice early. Free legal aid is given through the legal services authorities under NALSA. Women, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes members, children, persons with disabilities, victims of trafficking and people below the income limit are eligible. You can call NALSA's helpline at 15100. You can also get free legal advice before going to court through Tele-Law at a Common Service Centre in your village, or at https://tele-law.in/. Keep any papers about your land, so a lawyer can see what happened.
