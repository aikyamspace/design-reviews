I read the Supreme Court of India's judgment of 8 January 2020 in Vidya Devi v. The State of Himachal Pradesh & Ors., delivered by Justice Indu Malhotra, with Justice Ajay Rastogi on the bench. The case was about a woman whose land the State had taken for a road without acquiring it under any law, and who had received no compensation.

The facts. The appellant, then almost 80 years old, undisputedly owned about 3.34 hectares of land in Mauja Jalari, Tehsil Nadaun, District Hamirpur, Himachal Pradesh. In 1967–68 the State took over the land to build the Nadaun–Sujanpur major district road. It did so without acquisition proceedings and without following due process of law. The road was completed by 1975. The appellant was an illiterate widow from a rural background, unaware of her rights, and she filed no claim for compensation.

In 2004 other landowners whose land had been taken for the same road filed a writ petition in the Himachal Pradesh High Court (Anakh Singh & Ors. v. State of Himachal Pradesh). On 23 April 2007 the High Court allowed it and directed the State to acquire their lands under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. In 2008 the State began acquisition proceedings only for those petitioners' lands, not for other owners whose land had also been taken.

The appellant said she learnt of these proceedings in 2010. She and her two daughters then filed a writ petition in the High Court asking for compensation, or alternatively a direction to the State to initiate acquisition. The State admitted that it had used her land for the road in 1967–68 and had been in continuous possession for 42 years. It argued that its title had become one by "adverse possession", that her remedy was a civil suit, and that the petition was barred by laches. It also said she and her predecessors had verbally consented to the taking.

On 11 September 2013 the High Court held that the case raised disputed questions of law and fact about when limitation began, which could not be decided in writ proceedings. It gave the appellant liberty to file a civil suit. Her review petition was dismissed on 13 May 2014. She appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the Court decided. The Court allowed the appeals and set aside the High Court's orders of 11 September 2013 and 13 May 2014.

The Court's reasons.

  • Right to property. In 1967 the right to property was a fundamental right under Article 31, and a person could not be deprived of it without due process of law and just compensation. It ceased to be a fundamental right with the Constitution (Forty-Fourth Amendment) Act, 1978. But it remained a human right in a welfare State and a constitutional right under Article 300A, under which no person may be deprived of property save by authority of law. The Court said the obligation to pay compensation can be inferred in Article 300A. It relied on K.T. Plantation, Tukaram Kana Joshi, Hindustan Petroleum v. Darius Shapur Chenai, N. Padmamma, Delhi Airtech Services and Jilubhai Nanbhai Khachar. Forcibly dispossessing a person without following due process would violate both the human right and the Article 300A right.
  • No legal sanction. The appellant could not have been dispossessed without legal sanction or without compensation. The State's claim that she or her predecessors had "orally" consented was "completely baseless", and the Court found a complete lack of authority and legal sanction for the taking. In a democracy governed by the rule of law, the State must follow the procedure for acquisition, requisition or another permissible statutory mode.
  • Adverse possession. The Court said it was "surprised" by the State's plea. A welfare State cannot invoke adverse possession, a doctrine that lets a trespasser gain title after 12 years, to perfect its title over its own citizens' land.
  • Delay and laches. Delay and laches cannot be raised where the cause of action is a continuing one or where the circumstances shock the judicial conscience. There is no period of limitation for a constitutional court exercising its jurisdiction to do substantial justice. Relying on P.S. Sadasivaswamy and Tukaram Kana Joshi, the Court held that where the State took land without sanction of law, delay did not bar the claim. Here the appellant, an illiterate rural widow, had gone without any compensation for over half a century. The cause of action was continuing, and the demand for justice was compelling because the State itself admitted it took the land without any procedure known to law.
  • Unequal treatment. The State had begun acquisition only where the High Court directed it to, leaving other owners whose land had been taken for the same purpose at the same time to approach the courts one by one.

Directions. Exercising its powers under Articles 136 and 142 of the Constitution, the Court directed the State to:

  • pay compensation on the same terms as the Reference Court's order of 7 July 2015 in Anakh Singh's case (Land Reference No. 1 of 2011), with all statutory benefits including solatium and interest, within 8 weeks, treating the matter as a case of deemed acquisition;
  • file an affidavit of compliance within 10 weeks;
  • pay the appellant legal costs of Rs. 1,00,000 (the judgment printed this as "Rs.1,00,0000/-").

The Court noted that an appeal in that case (RFA No. 35 of 2016) was pending in the High Court at Shimla. It therefore provided that if the appellant filed an appeal within 8 weeks of receiving the compensation, it would be treated as within limitation and decided on its merits in accordance with law.
