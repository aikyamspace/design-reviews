I read the Supreme Court of India's judgment in Vidya Devi v. State of Himachal Pradesh, decided on 8 January 2020 by a bench of Justices Indu Malhotra and Ajay Rastogi.

The case. The appellant, a woman now close to 80, owned about 3.34 hectares of land in Himachal Pradesh. In 1967–68 the State took the land to build the Nadaun–Sujanpur district road, which was completed by 1975. It did not acquire the land under any law or follow any legal process, and it paid no compensation. The appellant was an illiterate widow from a rural background and did not know her rights, so she took no action for decades.

In 2004, other landowners whose land had been taken for the same road went to the High Court. In 2007 the High Court directed the State to acquire their land under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894. The State began acquisition in 2008, but only for those petitioners. The appellant learned of this in 2010 and filed her own writ petition, asking for compensation or, alternatively, for acquisition proceedings.

The State admitted it had used her land for the road. It argued that:

  • its possession of over 42 years had ripened into "adverse possession";
  • her remedy was a civil suit;
  • the petition was barred by delay and laches; and
  • she or her predecessors had orally consented to the taking.

In 2013 the High Court declined to decide the matter in writ proceedings. It said there were disputed questions of law and fact about when limitation began to run, and it gave her liberty to file a civil suit. It dismissed her review petition in 2014. She appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the Court decided. The Court allowed the appeals and set aside both High Court orders. It directed the State to pay compensation on the same terms as those awarded to Anakh Singh, one of the earlier petitioners, by the Reference Court in 2015. This was to include solatium, interest and other statutory benefits, and the case was to be treated as one of "deemed acquisition." Payment was due within eight weeks, with a compliance affidavit within ten weeks. The Court also ordered the State to pay her costs of Rs. 1,00,000. Because an appeal in Anakh Singh's case was pending in the High Court, the Court allowed her to file her own appeal within eight weeks of receiving payment, and it said that appeal would be treated as within time.

The Court's reasons.

  • Right to property. When the land was taken in 1967, the right to property was a fundamental right under Article 31 and could not be taken away without due process and compensation. It later ceased to be a fundamental right, but it remains a constitutional right under Article 300A and is also a human right. The State may deprive a person of property only under authority of law, and the Court said an obligation to pay compensation can be inferred from Article 300A. The Court relied on earlier decisions, including Tukaram Kana Joshi v. M.I.D.C. and others. Here there was no legal sanction at all.
  • Oral consent. The Court called the claim of oral consent completely baseless.
  • Adverse possession. The Court said it was surprised by this plea. Adverse possession lets a trespasser gain title, and a welfare State governed by the rule of law cannot use it to perfect its title over its own citizens' land.
  • Delay and laches. These cannot be raised where the cause of action is continuing or where the circumstances shock the judicial conscience. Constitutional courts have no fixed limitation period and can act to do substantial justice. Citing Tukaram Kana Joshi, the Court noted that in a case of land taken without legal authority the State must comply with the procedure for acquisition or another lawful mode.
  • The appellant's circumstances. She was an illiterate rural widow, deprived of her land without compensation for over half a century, and the State had admitted it took the land without any lawful procedure. The Court found the demand for justice compelling and used its powers under Articles 136 and 142 of the Constitution to order compensation.
  • Unequal treatment. The State acquired land only where the High Court had ordered it, which forced other owners whose land was taken at the same time for the same purpose to go to court individually.
