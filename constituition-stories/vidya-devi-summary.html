Case: Vidya Devi v. State of Himachal Pradesh & Ors. (Supreme Court of India, 8 January 2020)

What the case was about: Vidya Devi, now about 80, an illiterate widow from a rural area, owned about 3.34 hectares of land in Himachal Pradesh. In 1967–68 the State took her land to build the Nadaun–Sujanpur district road. It did not start any acquisition proceedings, follow any legal process, or pay her compensation. The road was finished by 1975. She did not know her rights and did not go to court at the time.

In 2004, other landowners whose land was taken for the same road went to the High Court. In 2007 the High Court ordered the State to acquire their land under the Land Acquisition Act, 1894, and the State did so in 2008, but only for those petitioners. Vidya Devi learned of this in 2010 and filed her own writ petition asking for compensation or for acquisition proceedings. The State admitted it had used her land but argued that:

  • it had held the land for over 42 years, so it had become the owner by "adverse possession";
  • she had delayed too long (laches);
  • she or her family had orally agreed to the taking;
  • her remedy was a civil suit.

In 2013 the High Court refused to decide the matter, saying there were disputed questions of fact and law, and told her to file a civil suit. Her review petition was dismissed in 2014. She appealed to the Supreme Court.

What the court decided: The Supreme Court allowed the appeals and set aside the High Court's orders. It ordered the State to:

  • pay her compensation on the same terms awarded to the neighbouring landowner Anakh Singh (by the Reference Court in 2015), together with solatium, interest and other statutory benefits, treating the case as a "deemed acquisition";
  • pay within 8 weeks and file an affidavit of compliance within 10 weeks;
  • pay her legal costs of Rs. 1,00,000.

If she is unhappy with the amount, she may appeal within 8 weeks of receiving payment, and that appeal will be treated as filed in time. A similar appeal by Anakh Singh's family is pending in the High Court.

Why:

  • Right to property: In 1967 the right to property was a fundamental right. It later became a constitutional right under Article 300A, which says no one can be deprived of property except by authority of law. The Court also described it as a human right. The State cannot take a citizen's land without legal authority and fair compensation.
  • Oral consent: The claim that she had agreed orally was baseless. There was no legal authority for the taking.
  • Adverse possession: The Court was "surprised" by this argument. A welfare State cannot use a doctrine meant for trespassers to take its own citizens' land and perfect its title.
  • Delay: Delay and laches do not bar a claim where the cause of action is continuing or where the circumstances shock the court's conscience. She had been without compensation for over 50 years, and the demand for justice was compelling. Courts exercising constitutional powers are not bound by fixed limitation periods, and the Court relied on Tukaram Kana Joshi v. MIDC.
  • Fairness: The State acted only for those landowners who went to court, forcing others to litigate individually. The Court used its extraordinary powers under Articles 136 and 142 of the Constitution to do complete justice and award compensation directly, rather than sending her to a civil suit.
